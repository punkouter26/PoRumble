PoRumble Docs

Sixteen boxers, one ring, last one standing

PoRumble is a top-down boxing game where sixteen fighters share one ring and brawl until a single one is left. Most of the fighters are controlled by a small computer brain that learned to box by practising against itself.

You can sit back and watch all sixteen fight (the DEMO), or take one fighter's place yourself (1 PLAYER). Either way you can back a fighter with play money before the bell, and the game keeps a league table of how every fighter has done over time.

It runs entirely on your own computer or phone. There is no account, no server and no internet connection involved in playing it.

Who it is for

The spectator

Presses DEMO, picks a favourite, and watches the fight like a sports broadcast with a commentator, live odds and a scoreboard.

The player

Presses 1 PLAYER and fights fifteen computer opponents with keyboard and mouse, or with touch on a phone.

The developer

Runs training sessions that teach the computer brain to box better, then ships the improved brain inside the game.

How big it is

Most of the code is the part you see and hear. The rules of boxing themselves are a small core.

The system at a glance

People and the computer brain both push the same buttons. The rules decide what happens, the scoreboard remembers it, and the screen shows it.

  1. A person, or the trained brain, asks a boxer to move or punch. Controls
  2. The rules decide whether the punch lands, is blocked, or misses. Rules
  3. Health and positions are updated on the live scoreboard. State
  4. The result is announced, and the screen, the crowd and the commentator react. Screens
  5. When the fight ends, the league table, the bank and the match diary are saved. Files

What people actually do with it

There are five things a person can set out to do: watch a fight, fight in one, back a fighter, walk away from a fight, and teach the computer to box. Each is told below as a short story.

The shape of every match

Every match moves through the same six screens in the same order, like rounds on a fight night programme.

1. Title

The menu. Waits for you; no clock.

2. Introduction

"Get ready." Lasts 0.8 seconds.

3. Countdown

3, 2, 1. Lasts 3 seconds. Nobody can punch yet.

4. Fight

Live boxing until one is left, or 3 minutes pass.

5. Knockout hold

1.6 seconds of slow motion on the final blow.

6. Results

Who won. Rematch or return to the menu.

Timings are hardcoded constants, not settings: Assets/Scripts/Systems/Match/MatchFlowSystem.cs:25-34. The phase list is Assets/Scripts/Models/Match/MatchFlowModel.cs:16-38.

Journey 1: Watch a demo fight

You press one button and sixteen computer-controlled boxers fight it out while you watch.

  1. You open the game and see the title screen with the sixteen fighters on the card. Screen
  2. You press DEMO. Screen
  3. The front desk checks that the menu is showing and that no finished match is still on the table, then starts the introduction. Rules
  4. After the countdown the bell rings and every boxer starts taking instructions from the trained brain. AI
  5. Each time a boxer is knocked out, the referee counts who is left. When one remains, the match is over. Rules
  6. The league table is updated and saved, and one line is added to the match diary. Files
  7. The results screen appears. Screen

Journey 3: Back a fighter

Before the bell you tap the fighter you think will win. If they do, your play-money bank grows; if not, you lose your stake.

  1. On the title screen you tap a fighter's tile. Tapping it again clears the pick. Screen
  2. Each tile shows a price based on the fighter's league rating: long shots pay more. Rules
  3. When the fight is introduced, 100 points leave your bank and the price is locked. Changing your mind mid-fight cannot move the bet. Rules
  4. If your bank had fallen below 100, it is topped back up to 1,000 first, so you are never shut out. Rules
  5. Your fighter wins: you are paid the stake times the locked price. They lose: you get nothing back. Rules
  6. A draw, or leaving through MENU, returns the stake. Rules
  7. The bank is saved to a file so it is still there next time. Your pick itself is not saved. Files

Journey 4: A fight that will not end

If boxers keep running away from each other, the ring itself shrinks until they cannot, and after three minutes a bell ends it.

  1. For the first 60 seconds the ring is full size. Rules
  2. From 60 to 120 seconds the ropes slide in until the ring is 30% of its original size. Rules
  3. The boxers get one more minute in the small ring to settle it themselves. Rules
  4. At 180 seconds the bell rings. Whoever has the most health left wins; an exact tie is a draw. Rules

The bell is rung by Assets/Scripts/Views/Composition/MatchDirector.cs:218-221 and resolved by Assets/Scripts/Systems/Match/MatchSystem.cs:55-63; ties resolve to no winner at Assets/Scripts/Models/Match/MatchModel.cs:71-99.

Journey 5: Walk away, or go again

You can always get back to the menu, but you cannot accidentally skip or cut short a fight by mashing a key.

  1. During a fight, the MENU button in the top bar abandons it and returns to the title screen. Any stake is refunded. Screen
  2. On the results screen, Enter starts a rematch with the same seats; R returns to the menu so you can change your pick. Controls
  3. Pressing Enter or R at any other time does nothing: the front desk refuses requests that do not fit the current screen. Rules

Journey 6: Teach the computer to box

A developer runs thousands of fast, screenless practice fights. After each one the brain is told how well it did, and it gradually gets better.

  1. The developer starts the progress dashboard (TensorBoard), then the Python trainer, then presses Play in a training scene. Developer
  2. A training scene has no menu, no countdown and no HUD: the fight is live from the first instant. Rules
  3. Boxers earn small rewards for landing punches, dodging and blocking, and pay small costs for being hit or wasting punches. AI
  4. When a practice fight ends, the winner gets a bonus (bigger for a knockout than for winning on the bell), and the ring is reset at once for the next fight. AI
  5. The trainer saves the improved brain as a file, which the developer copies into the game. Developer

Training detection: Assets/Scripts/Views/Composition/MatchDirector.cs:101, live-from-first-step at Assets/Scripts/Views/Composition/MatchDirector.cs:128-131, episode reset at Assets/Scripts/Views/Composition/MatchDirector.cs:243-266. The AI report covers rewards in detail.

How the pieces fit together

The game is built in three layers, like a boxing arena: the scoreboard that holds the facts, the officials who apply the rules, and the broadcast crew who show and voice it all. Each layer is only allowed to talk downward.

Broadcast crew Views

Everything you see, hear and touch: boxers on screen, the HUD, cameras, crowd, controls, and the file drawers. 53 files.

Officials Systems

The rules. Who hit whom, when the match ends, how ratings move, what a pick pays. 20 files, no screens.

Scoreboard Models

The facts right now: each boxer's health and position, the match phase, the league table. 50 files, no logic that needs a screen.

Two helpers tie the layers together. The Tool Shed hands every part the things it needs when the scene starts. The PA System lets the officials announce events without knowing who is listening.

Component map

A map of the building: who works on which floor, and which doors exist between floors.

  1. When the scene loads, the Tool Shed builds every official and every scoreboard exactly once and hands them out. Views
  2. The Assembly Line runs each physics step: it asks the officials to move boxers, then asks the Referee whether the match is over. Views to Systems
  3. Officials never call the screens. They change the scoreboard and make announcements. Systems to Models
  4. Screens listen: to the scoreboard for values (health bars), and to the PA system for moments (a punch landed, play the sound). Models to Views
  5. Only three officials touch storage, and only through a narrow "save / load" contract, so the rules never know where a file lives. Systems to Files

Request path: a punch becomes a knockout

One punch, followed from the button press to the league table. This is the main road through the whole system.

  1. A person or the trained brain asks for a punch. Both arrive through the same controls component. Controls
  2. The movement official starts the punch only if no other fist is already travelling, and announces "punch thrown". Rules
  3. Pure geometry decides the outcome: landed, blocked by a glove, or slipped. No physics engine is involved, so the same inputs always give the same result. Rules
  4. The damage official hears "punch landed", removes health, and pushes the target backwards. At zero health it announces a knockout, exactly once per boxer. Rules
  5. After the step, the Referee counts survivors. Fewer than two means the match is over. Rules
  6. "Match ended" is heard by the league table, the bank and the diary, which each save their own file. Files

Data model

What the game remembers. The top half lives only while the game is open; the three shaded "file" boxes at the bottom are what is still there tomorrow.

  1. A match holds a list of boxers. Each boxer is a seat number plus live facts: health, stamina, position, two arms. State
  2. The roster decides which contestant sits in which seat. A contestant is a tuning sheet: a name, a look, and optionally its own trained brain. State
  3. The league table keeps one record per contestant, found by the contestant's id, so a fighter keeps their rating whichever chair they sit in. State
  4. Values a screen needs to follow (health, phase, bank) are "reactive": changing one automatically notifies whoever is watching. State to Screens
  5. Only three things are written to disk: the league table, the bank totals, and one diary line per match. Files

Storage schema as written

There is no SQL or ORM. Persistence is Unity's JsonUtility over private serializable classes, under Application.persistentDataPath.

Whole-file saves go through a write-beside-then-swap helper so a kill mid-save cannot leave a half-written file: Assets/Scripts/Views/Persistence/AtomicFile.cs:16-29. Request path: one practice fight during training

Training uses the same rules with the broadcast crew sent home. The trainer is a separate program that watches and adjusts the brain.

  1. Each boxer reports what it can sense to the trainer and receives an action back. AI
  2. The action goes through the same rules as in the real game. Rules
  3. If nobody wins in time, the bell falls five steps before the trainer's own limit so the result is recorded in the right fight. Rules
  4. The winner is paid, every boxer's episode is closed, and the ring is reset immediately. AI

The AI, and everything outside the game

The "AI" in PoRumble is a small file that ships inside the game and decides how each computer boxer moves and punches. It is not a chatbot and it never talks to the internet. Nothing you do while playing is sent anywhere.

Cloud AI services

None. No calls to any online AI provider exist in the game code.

Cost per fight

Nothing. The brain runs on your own device, so there is no usage bill.

If the internet is down

No effect. The game never needs a connection.

What the boxing brain does

Ten times a second each computer boxer looks around, and the brain answers with where to step, where to face, and whether to throw a left or a right. That is all it can do.

It sees

Its own health, breath, arms and position, plus sight-lines that report faces, bodies and walls nearby.

It decides

Four dials (step left/right, step forward/back, aim left/right, aim up/down) and two switches (left punch, right punch).

It cannot

Change the rules, see anything it is not given, act before the bell, or throw two punches at once.

  1. The boxer gathers its fifteen numbers and its sight-line readings. Controls
  2. The brain file turns them into four dials and two switches. This runs on the device. AI
  3. Each dial is clipped to the range -1 to 1 before use, so a wild output cannot make a boxer move faster than the rules allow. Controls
  4. A fighter's personality (aggressive, cautious, likes haymakers) is layered on top by ordinary code, not by the brain. Rules
  5. The request goes to the same rules a human player's input goes to. Rules

Who can do what, and what is kept

PoRumble has no login. There are no accounts, no passwords and no security passes, because the game runs on one device for whoever is holding it and never talks to a server. What it does have is a strict set of house rules about when each action is allowed.

Accounts and passwords

None exist. Nothing asks who you are.

Personal data collected

None. The saved files hold fighter ratings, play-money totals and fight statistics.

Data sent off the device

None. The game code makes no network calls.

Real money

None. The bank is play points that refill for free when they run out.

The Visitor Badge auth tokens
Not used. Think of a board game on your kitchen table: whoever is sitting there can play. Proposed / Not Yet Implemented: sign-in, user profiles, cloud saves, online leaderboards.
The Security Checkpoint request guards
This does exist, in a different form. Every request passes the front desk, which checks one thing: is this allowed on the screen we are on right now? In code: the phase guards on MatchFlowSystem and PredictionSystem.
The Filing Cabinet storage
Three plain files in the app's own data folder. Anyone with access to the device's files can read or edit them. They are not locked because nothing in them is worth locking.
